Dungeons and Dragons has been a part of my life for the better part of 6 years, playing with a variety of groups, some as a player, but most as a DM. 

As a player, I've played a variety of characters: From my first character Winter, a dragonborn fighter with the soldier background, to my most recent character, a homebrewed Gnoll [Illrigger](https://shop.mcdmproductions.com/products/illrigger-class). Although my character ideas have gotten more ambitious over the years, there has been one constant, they were all for campaigns that never completed, or one shots. 

I saw this as a problem. I wanted to complete a campaign, and I wanted to have character growth over it's full duration, but with each new character rolled up I knew it was for naught. One day, I eventually decided to change this. I volunteered to run a campaign. For those unaware, having someone who is willing to DM a campaign is rarer than finding a specific grain of sand in the Atlantic Ocean. But at the time I didn't know why this was. I very quickly learned why: DMing is *hard*.

To most players, they see their DM as someone who shows up once a week for three hours to play along with them. To the DM, each session is a culmination of hours of prep time that week, paired with even more hours of time spent planning weeks in advance. Even during the game DM's can't catch a break. They have to juggle multiple NPCs, statblocks for combat, story progression, and above all, manage the unpredictability of the players themselves. The players are the greatest enemy, all your effort could fly out the window due to one player asking what the random guard's name is.

Unfortunately, I was up for that challenge. After completing one campaign spanning a year, and currently DMing a campaign for 2 years now, I've learned a lot. There have been many highs, but there have also been many lows. That being said, I hope to share some tips and tricks for other DM's to avoid these pitfalls.
# Don't Be Afraid To Use An Official Adventure
Using an official adventure has been an invaluable tool to me for running a long term campaign. It allows me to have a coherent pre-created story, while allowing the flexibility to add and expand content to my heart's content. Don't like something? Just change it! Need to make an encounter harder? Just change it! Want to add a side quest? Just add it!

Having a base to work off of is far better than just winging it. My year long campaign was entirely homebrewed, but the one thing I wish I had was a fallback. You may think that you have infinite ideas to make a story off of, but most likely, you don't. That, or one day you really just don't feel like making something up. That's what the adventure is for, it provides you a fallback story if you don't have the time to make something up that day, but doesn't lock you into anything.
# Plan Ahead, But Don't Commit To It
D&D isn't a video game, it's a space for you and your friends to have fun. Your players are going to do unexpected things, and as a DM it's your job to be able to adapt, and do what is most fun for your players. It sucks when something you've been planning for months in advance doesn't work out, and it sucks when your players don't enjoy something you've planned hours into. I've experienced this multiple times myself. Over your campaign, you should idly gauge what your players enjoy, and adapt as you go. Planning too far in advance can cause you to get locked into a certain idea, and often you don't realize that it doesn't work right when it matters most: During the session. The players look up to you, and you should make their experience as smooth as possible.
# Have Fun Outside The Session
D&D adventures shouldn't be limited to just the session. I found that giving your players little puzzles to solve can be a good way to test their memory, especially when the hints or clues are based around campaign happenings. To make their effort worthwhile, my rewards are pieces of lore for them to connect to the main plot. Overall, it can be fun to make, and watch your players try to solve.

I've also loved making visuals for my party. Once, I made a whole newspaper to help tie events together, grounding their actions and choices in the world around them.

# Your Players Care
The reality is, you have poured far more time and effort into the campaign than your players have. But that's not a bad thing. If your players show up each week, they do care. They could have been doing anything else during this time, but they chose to come to your session. When the going gets tough, just remember that your players are there for you, and they want to see what story you have to tell.
